Overview

My research concerns quantitative limit theory for random spatial structures and has three connected strands.

Distributional approximation and Stein’s method

Many quantities in stochastic geometry and random graphs are sums of local contributions with intricate dependence, and the basic question is how close their distribution is to a limit law, in an explicit probability metric. For Gaussian limits, we have developed a Malliavin–Stein framework for Poisson functionals that stabilize on regions other than balls, which makes models with long-range interactions accessible, such as minimal points of a Poisson process and birth–growth models [6]. A second line concerns non-Gaussian limits, in particular the Dickman distribution, which appears for weighted sums in probabilistic number theory, the analysis of algorithms and random trees; here we obtained the first approximation bounds in the Kolmogorov distance [8].

Superconcentration, chaos and noise sensitivity

The second strand asks how large fluctuations are and how they react to small perturbations of the underlying randomness. The Poincaré inequality gives a general upper bound on the variance of a Poisson functional, and a functional is called superconcentrated if its variance is of smaller order. We show that superconcentration is equivalent to chaos, that is, to decorrelation under a small perturbation by the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (spatial birth–death) dynamics, a Poisson analogue of Chatterjee’s theory for Gaussian fields [11]. For crossing events in continuum percolation, we introduce a spectral point process of a Poisson functional, the counterpart of the spectral sample of a Boolean function, and use it to prove sharp noise sensitivity in planar Poisson–Voronoi percolation and sharp noise instability in the critical Poisson Boolean model [13].

Applications to nonparametric and causal inference

Procedures based on nearest neighbours and data-adaptive partitions produce outputs that are stabilizing functionals of the sample, so the same tools quantify how accurate the Gaussian approximations used for inference are, in terms of the sample size and tuning parameters. We prove multivariate Gaussian approximation bounds for random forest predictions based on potential nearest neighbours, via a general result for region-based stabilizing functionals [12], and Gaussian and bootstrap approximation bounds for matching-based estimators of average treatment effects, which yield non-asymptotically valid confidence intervals [9].
